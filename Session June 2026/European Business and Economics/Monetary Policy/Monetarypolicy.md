# Monetary Policy — EU & ECB
> How the ECB manages the euro and what it means for businesses and economies

---

## PART 1 — COMPLETE SYNTHESIS

## KEY CONCEPT 1 — What Is Monetary Policy?

Monetary policy = the set of tools used by a central bank to manage the **money supply and interest rates** in order to achieve macroeconomic objectives (price stability, growth, employment).

**Two transmission channels:**
1. **Interest rate channel**: raising rates → borrowing more expensive → less spending → inflation falls
2. **Credit channel**: raising rates → banks lend less → less money in economy → deflation/disinflation

**Conventional monetary policy:** Adjusting short-term interest rates.
**Unconventional monetary policy:** When rates hit zero — quantitative easing (QE), forward guidance, negative rates.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 2 — The ECB: Structure and Independence

**European Central Bank (ECB)**
- Founded: June 1, 1998 (during Eurozone Phase 2)
- Location: Frankfurt am Main, Germany
- President: Christine Lagarde (since 2019)

**Governing bodies:**
| Body | Composition | Role |
|------|-------------|------|
| **Governing Council** | ECB Executive Board (6) + 20 national central bank governors | Main decision-making body: sets interest rates |
| **Executive Board** | President + Vice-President + 4 members (8-year terms) | Implements decisions, manages daily operations |
| **General Council** | President + VP + all 27 NCB governors (not just eurozone) | Coordination with non-euro states |

**Independence:** The ECB is completely independent from political pressure. No government or EU institution can instruct the ECB. This is enshrined in Article 130 TFEU. Purpose: central bank independence → credibility → lower inflation expectations.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 3 — The ECB's Mandate (Single vs Dual)

**ECB's primary mandate:** Price stability = keeping inflation at **2% over the medium term** (symmetric target — equally concerned about going above AND below 2%).

**ECB ≠ FED:** The US Federal Reserve has a **dual mandate**: price stability + full employment. The ECB focuses exclusively on inflation. However, the ECB can support the EU's general economic policies "without prejudice to the objective of price stability" (Art. 127 TFEU).

**Why 2%?**
- High enough to avoid deflation trap
- Low enough to prevent erosion of purchasing power
- Buffer for relative price adjustments between eurozone countries

---

## KEY CONCEPT 4 — The ECB's 3 Key Interest Rates

| Rate | Also called | What it does |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Main refinancing rate (MRO)** | Policy rate | Rate at which banks borrow from ECB for 1 week. Sets the "price of money" in the economy |
| **Marginal lending facility** | Ceiling rate | Overnight borrowing for banks with emergency liquidity needs. Higher than MRO |
| **Deposit facility rate** | Floor rate | Rate ECB pays on excess reserves banks park overnight. Lower than MRO |

**The interest rate corridor:**
```
Marginal lending facility (ceiling) — e.g., 4.75%
        ↑
Main refinancing rate (MRO) — e.g., 4.50%
        ↓
Deposit facility (floor) — e.g., 4.00%
```

**Real-world example (2022-2023):** Post-COVID inflation hit ~10% across the eurozone. ECB raised all 3 rates aggressively: MRO from 0% to 4.5%, deposit facility from -0.5% to 4%. This was the fastest rate-hiking cycle in ECB history.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 5 — Conventional Monetary Policy Tools

**Expansionary (loose) policy** — used to stimulate economy, fight deflation:
- Lower interest rates
- Increase money supply through open market operations (buying bonds)
- Lower reserve requirements

**Contractionary (tight) policy** — used to fight inflation:
- Raise interest rates
- Reduce money supply (selling bonds, reducing balance sheet)
- Raise reserve requirements

**Open Market Operations (OMOs):** The ECB's main tool. Buys or sells government bonds in the market to inject or withdraw liquidity. The "repo rate" (MRO) is the rate at which banks can borrow from ECB against collateral.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 6 — Unconventional Monetary Policy

When conventional tools are exhausted (interest rates at zero or negative):

**Quantitative Easing (QE):**
- ECB buys large amounts of government and corporate bonds
- Injects liquidity directly into financial system
- Pushes down long-term interest rates
- ECB launched major QE programs: APP (2015), PEPP (2020 — COVID pandemic emergency)

**Forward Guidance:**
- ECB communicates its future policy intentions
- Reduces uncertainty: "rates will stay low for an extended period"
- Shapes market expectations without changing rates immediately

**Negative Interest Rates (NIRP):**
- Deposit facility rate went negative (to -0.5%) from 2014-2022
- Banks penalized for parking money at ECB → incentive to lend
- Novel tool with uncertain side effects (bank profitability squeeze)

**PEPP (Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme, 2020):**
- €1,850 billion asset purchase program
- Flexible — could buy more bonds from countries hit harder by COVID
- Prevented Italy/Spain sovereign debt crisis during pandemic

## KEY CONCEPT 7 — The Transmission Mechanism

How ECB rate decisions affect the real economy:

```
ECB raises rates
      ↓
Banks' borrowing costs rise
      ↓
Banks raise rates on loans to companies and households
      ↓
Less credit demand → less investment → less consumption
      ↓
GDP growth slows → labor market cools → wages stable
      ↓
Inflation falls back toward 2%
```

**Time lag:** Monetary policy acts with a lag of 12-18 months. The ECB must anticipate future inflation, not just react to current data.

**Transmission problems in the Eurozone:**
- 20 different banking systems with varying efficiency
- Different housing markets (variable vs fixed rate mortgages)
- Fiscal policy varies — Germany may respond differently to rate hikes than Italy

---

## KEY CONCEPT 8 — Mundell's Trilemma and the ECB

The ECB exists because of a fundamental choice made under Mundell's trilemma:

**You cannot simultaneously have:**
1. Fixed exchange rate
2. Autonomous monetary policy
3. Free capital movement

**The Eurozone chose:** Fixed exchange rate (euro is pegged across member states — actually eliminated exchange rates) + free capital movement.

**The price:** All 20 eurozone members surrendered their independent monetary policy to the ECB.

**Structural tension:** The ECB sets ONE interest rate for 20 very different economies. What's right for Germany (booming, needs higher rates) may hurt Greece (in recession, needs lower rates). This is the fundamental structural challenge of the EMU.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 9 — ECB vs National Central Banks (ESCB)

The **ESCB (European System of Central Banks)** = ECB + all 27 national central banks (including non-eurozone).

The **Eurosystem** = ECB + 20 eurozone national central banks (e.g., Banque de France, Bundesbank, Banca d'Italia).

**How it works in practice:**
- ECB Governing Council sets the policy
- National central banks implement it in their country (buy bonds, provide liquidity to local banks)
- National central banks are the "operational arm" of the ECB

**KEY DISTINCTION:** National central banks no longer set monetary policy — they execute ECB decisions. But they retain functions like:
- Bank supervision (with ECB coordination via SSM)
- Coin issuance
- Statistical data collection

---

## KEY CONCEPT 10 — Macro-Prudential Policy and the SSM

**Macro-prudential policy:** Ensuring the stability of the ENTIRE financial system, not just individual banks.

- **Micro-prudential**: Supervising individual banks (are they solvent?)
- **Macro-prudential**: Monitoring systemic risks (could one bank's failure cause a chain reaction?)

**SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism, created 2014):**
- ECB directly supervises ~120 "significant" European banks
- National supervisors handle less significant banks under ECB oversight
- Created after 2008 crisis to prevent another systemic meltdown

**Tools:** Capital buffers (counter-cyclical), loan-to-value limits, leverage ratio requirements.

**Banking Union (3 pillars):**
1. SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism) — supervision ✓ done
2. SRM (Single Resolution Mechanism) — resolving failing banks ✓ done
3. EDIS (European Deposit Insurance Scheme) — guaranteeing deposits ✗ still incomplete

## PART 2 — Q&A EXAM FORMAT

**Q1: What is the ECB's mandate? How does it differ from the US Federal Reserve?**
> A: The ECB has a single mandate: price stability, defined as inflation at 2% over the medium term (symmetric — equally concerned about inflation above AND below target). The US Federal Reserve (FED) has a dual mandate: price stability AND maximum employment. The ECB can support general EU economic policies but only if it doesn't compromise price stability.

**Q2: What are the 3 interest rates controlled by the ECB? What does each one do?**
> A: 1. Main refinancing rate (MRO): rate at which banks borrow from ECB for one week — this is the "price of money" affecting all lending rates. 2. Marginal lending facility: overnight emergency borrowing rate for banks — always above MRO (ceiling). 3. Deposit facility rate: rate paid on banks' excess reserves parked at ECB overnight — always below MRO (floor).

**Q3: How does raising ECB interest rates reduce inflation?**
> A: Higher ECB rates → banks' borrowing costs rise → banks raise rates on loans to households and companies → credit becomes more expensive → less consumption and investment → economic activity slows → labor market cools → wages stabilize → inflation falls. This transmission takes 12-18 months to work through the economy.

**Q4: What is the difference between conventional and unconventional monetary policy?**
> A: Conventional: adjusting short-term interest rates (MRO, deposit facility) to influence borrowing costs and economic activity. Unconventional: used when rates reach zero or the lower bound — includes Quantitative Easing (buying bonds to inject liquidity), Forward Guidance (communicating future policy intentions), and Negative Interest Rates (charging banks for parking reserves at ECB).

**Q5: What is Quantitative Easing (QE)? Give an EU example.**
> A: QE is when a central bank buys large quantities of assets (government bonds, corporate bonds) to inject liquidity into the financial system and push down long-term interest rates. EU example: the ECB launched the APP (Asset Purchase Programme) in 2015 and the PEPP (Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme) in 2020 with a €1,850 billion envelope to counter the COVID economic shock.

**Q6: What is Mundell's trilemma and how does it explain the ECB's existence?**
> A: A country cannot simultaneously have: fixed exchange rate, autonomous monetary policy, and free capital movement. The Eurozone chose fixed rates (euro eliminates intra-zone exchange rates) + free capital movement → all 20 members surrendered independent monetary policy to the ECB. One central bank sets rates for all.

**Q7: Why is the ECB's independence important?**
> A: An independent central bank is more credible because it cannot be pressured by governments to print money for political reasons (e.g., before elections). Credibility → anchored inflation expectations → lower inflation. Enshrined in Article 130 TFEU: no government or EU institution can instruct the ECB. This mirrors the model of the German Bundesbank, widely seen as the most credible pre-ECB central bank.

**Q8: What is the ESCB and how does it differ from the Eurosystem?**
> A: ESCB (European System of Central Banks) = ECB + all 27 national central banks, including non-eurozone states. Eurosystem = ECB + 20 eurozone national central banks only — this is the operational monetary policy system for the euro area. National central banks implement ECB decisions but no longer set independent monetary policy.

**Q9: What structural problem does the ECB face in the Eurozone?**
> A: One interest rate for 20 very different economies. What stabilizes inflation in Germany (booming, needs tighter policy) may deepen recession in Greece or Italy. Without fiscal transfers between states, countries in crisis cannot use monetary policy to adjust. This is the core tension of EMU — monetary union without full political and fiscal union. Greece in 2010 couldn't devalue or set its own rates.

**Q10: What is macro-prudential policy and who manages it in the EU?**
> A: Macro-prudential policy aims to preserve the stability of the ENTIRE financial system, not just individual banks. It monitors systemic risks: excessive credit growth, asset bubbles, leverage, contagion risk. Managed by the ECB through the SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism, est. 2014). The ECB directly supervises ~120 significant European banks.

**Q11: What is the SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism)?**
> A: The SSM is the system of banking supervision in the Eurozone, created in 2014 after the 2008 crisis. The ECB directly supervises "significant" banks (large, cross-border), while national supervisors handle smaller banks under ECB oversight. Goal: break the "doom loop" between weak banks and weak sovereigns that amplified the eurozone debt crisis.

**Q12: What is the Banking Union? How complete is it?**
> A: The EU's framework for integrated banking governance, with 3 planned pillars: 1. SSM (Single Supervisory Mechanism) — completed 2014. 2. SRM (Single Resolution Mechanism) — completed 2016, resolves failing banks without taxpayer bailouts. 3. EDIS (European Deposit Insurance Scheme) — NOT YET completed, politically blocked because richer northern countries don't want to guarantee southern banks' deposits.

**Q13: What happened to ECB policy during the 2022 inflation surge?**
> A: Post-COVID, inflation in the eurozone hit ~10% in 2022, driven by energy price shocks (Ukraine war) and supply chain disruptions. The ECB responded with the fastest rate-hiking cycle in its history: rates went from near-zero (deposit facility at -0.5%) to 4% in about 14 months (July 2022 – September 2023). This reduced inflation back toward 2% by 2024, but at the cost of slower growth.

**Q14: What is forward guidance and why does the ECB use it?**
> A: Forward guidance is communication by the ECB about its future policy intentions. Example: "We expect interest rates to remain at current levels for an extended period." This shapes market expectations before any action is taken, reducing uncertainty and influencing long-term interest rates. It's a powerful communication tool, especially when rates are near zero and conventional tools are limited.

**Q15: What is depreciation vs devaluation? Why can't eurozone countries devalue?**
> A: Depreciation = spontaneous market-driven fall in a currency's value (e.g., investor panic causes euro to fall). Devaluation = deliberate government/central bank decision to lower currency value to boost exports and restore competitiveness. Eurozone countries CANNOT devalue individually because they share the euro — the ECB manages the euro's external value for all 20 members. This is why Greece couldn't devalue in 2010 to regain export competitiveness.
