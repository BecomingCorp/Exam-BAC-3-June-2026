# EU Policies — Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)
> The EU's oldest and largest policy: feeding Europe and managing rural land

---

## PART 1 — COMPLETE SYNTHESIS

## KEY CONCEPT 1 — What Is the CAP and Why Does It Exist?

The **Common Agricultural Policy (CAP)** is the EU's agricultural support system — and historically its **largest budget item**.

**Origins (1962):** Created by the Treaty of Rome (1957), launched in 1962. Original goals post-WWII:
1. Guarantee food security (never again post-war food shortages)
2. Ensure a fair standard of living for farmers
3. Stabilize agricultural markets
4. Ensure availability of supplies at reasonable prices to consumers

**Managed by:** **DG AGRI** (Directorate-General for Agriculture and Rural Development)

**Budget:** ~31-33% of the total EU budget (~€55-58 billion/year in the 2021-2027 MFF). Historically was up to 70% of the EU budget in the 1970s — relative share has declined significantly.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 2 — The 2 Pillars of the CAP

The CAP is structured around two complementary pillars:

| Pillar | Name | What it funds | Funding |
|--------|------|--------------|---------|
| **Pillar 1** | Direct payments + Market measures | Income support for farmers; intervention in agricultural markets | 100% EU-funded |
| **Pillar 2** | Rural Development (EAFRD) | Environment, modernization, diversification, quality of life in rural areas | Shared (EU + national co-financing) |

**Pillar 1 — Direct Payments:**
- **Basic Payment Scheme (BPS)**: payment per hectare of agricultural land
- **Green Payment**: 30% of direct payments conditional on environmental practices (crop diversification, permanent pastures, ecological focus areas)
- **Young Farmer Payment**: additional support for farmers under 40
- **Redistributive Payment**: higher payments for first hectares (helps small farms)

**Pillar 2 — Rural Development:**
- Agri-environment-climate measures (AECM)
- Organic farming support
- Infrastructure investment (roads, broadband in rural areas)
- Leader programme (local community development)
- Training and advisory services for farmers

---

## KEY CONCEPT 3 — The Evolution of the CAP (Historical Reforms)

The CAP has been reformed repeatedly to fix its problems:

| Period | Reform | Key change |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **1962-1992** | Original CAP | Price supports + export subsidies → massive overproduction ("butter mountains," "wine lakes") |
| **1992** | MacSharry Reform | Shift from price support to direct aid; production limits introduced |
| **1999** | Agenda 2000 | Further cuts to price support; rural development as 2nd pillar created |
| **2003** | Fischler Reform | **Decoupling**: payments no longer linked to production → farmers free to produce what the market demands |
| **2013** | CAP 2014-2020 | "Greening" of Pillar 1 (30% conditional on environmental practices); convergence of payment rates |
| **2023** | CAP 2023-2027 | New delivery model: member states write Strategic Plans; stronger conditionality; eco-schemes |

**Key concept: Decoupling (2003)**
Before 2003: the more you produced, the more you received → perverse incentive. After 2003: payments based on land (area), not production → farmers produce what the market wants, not what maximizes subsidies.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 4 — The New CAP 2023-2027 (and Looking Toward 2028)

**New Delivery Model:**
- Member states write their own **CAP Strategic Plans** (approved by Commission)
- More flexibility to tailor CAP to national/regional conditions
- But common EU targets must be met

**9 Specific Objectives:**
1. Support viable farm income
2. Enhance market orientation and competitiveness
3. Improve farmers' position in the value chain
4. Contribute to climate change mitigation and adaptation
5. Foster sustainable development and natural resources management
6. Contribute to biodiversity protection
7. Attract young farmers
8. Promote employment in rural areas
9. Improve response to societal demands on food and health

**Eco-schemes (new instrument):**
- Voluntary for farmers, mandatory for member states to offer
- Rewarding practices beyond basic greening: precision farming, agroforestry, wetland restoration
- At least 25% of direct payments envelope allocated to eco-schemes

**CAP 2028 (incoming):**
- Even stronger environmental conditionality
- Integration with Green Deal targets
- Debate: balancing food security with climate goals

## KEY CONCEPT 5 — The Green Deal vs. Farmers: The 2024 Conflict

**The tension:** The European Green Deal requires significant changes to farming practices that farmers say make their operations unprofitable.

**Farm to Fork Strategy (F2F):**
- Reduce pesticide use by 50% by 2030
- Reduce fertilizer use by 20% by 2030
- Convert 25% of farmland to organic by 2030
- Reduce antibiotic use in livestock by 50%

**Why farmers protested (2024):**
- Tractor blockades across Brussels, Paris, Warsaw, Rome, Madrid
- Complaints: too much regulation, too little income support, unfair competition from cheaper non-EU imports (Ukraine grain, South American beef)
- EU standards are higher (environmental, animal welfare) but EU farmers compete with lower-standard imports
- Specific anger: "mirror clauses" — EU imports should meet same standards as EU production

**Commission response:** Partially rolled back some F2F targets; extended derogations on ecological focus areas; accelerated "simplification" of CAP rules.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 6 — Market Management: What Happens When Prices Crash?

Beyond direct payments, the CAP includes market management tools to handle extreme price volatility:

**Intervention mechanism:**
- If market prices fall below a reference price → EU can buy into public storage (intervention buying)
- Creates a "floor" for wheat, butter, skimmed milk powder prices
- Controversial: can distort markets and create surpluses

**Export subsidies:** Largely phased out under WTO pressure (historically distorted world food markets by dumping cheap EU products).

**Exceptional crisis measures:**
- Market disruption following shocks (COVID, Ukraine war)
- Commission can deploy the Agricultural Reserve (€450M in 2021-2027 MFF) for emergency measures

---

## KEY CONCEPT 7 — CAP and International Trade Tensions

**WTO and the CAP:**
- WTO rules limit trade-distorting agricultural subsidies ("amber box")
- EU direct payments are classified as "green box" (minimally trade-distorting) — key for WTO compatibility
- This is why decoupling from production was essential: production-linked subsidies are amber box

**Mercosur deal challenge:**
- EU-Mercosur trade deal (Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay) would lower tariffs on South American beef, sugar, chicken
- EU farmers (especially French) fiercely opposed: lower-standard imports compete with higher-standard EU production
- Deal stalled for years due to agricultural resistance

**Ukraine grain:**
- After Russian invasion (2022), EU suspended import tariffs on Ukrainian grain to support Ukraine
- Polish, Hungarian, Bulgarian, Romanian farmers blocked borders — massive grain influx at low prices destroyed local markets
- EU had to impose temporary safeguard measures and compensate affected farmers

---

## KEY CONCEPT 8 — The Circular Economy and Agriculture

The EU's circular economy ambitions directly affect the CAP:

**Circular principles in farming:**
- Precision agriculture: GPS-guided machinery, sensors → less waste, less chemical use
- Bioeconomy: using agricultural waste as bio-based materials or energy
- Composting and anaerobic digestion of food waste → back to farms as soil amendment
- Short supply chains: reducing food miles, connecting local producers to local consumers

**Biodiversity protection:**
- Natura 2000 network: ~18% of EU territory protected under Habitats and Birds Directives
- Farmers in these areas must manage land compatibly with conservation objectives
- Paid through Pillar 2 agri-environment schemes

---

## KEY CONCEPT 9 — Who Benefits from the CAP? Distributive Issues

**Concentration problem:**
- 80% of CAP subsidies go to 20% of farms (largest farms receive most area-based payments)
- Small farms in eastern and southern Europe receive proportionally less
- This has driven consolidation: fewer, larger farms → depopulation of rural areas

**Reforms to address this:**
- Capping payments (voluntary for member states — limit on large farms)
- Redistributive payment: higher per-hectare rate for first 150 hectares
- Degressivity: progressive reduction of payments above €60,000/farm
- Convergence: gradually equalizing payment rates between member states (eastern EU received less)

**North vs South vs East tension:**
- France and Germany: large farms, receive most payments, resist reform
- Mediterranean states (Italy, Spain): smaller, more diverse farms; want more market tools
- Central-Eastern Europe (Poland, Romania): historically lower payment rates; pushing for full convergence

## PART 2 — Q&A EXAM FORMAT

**Q1: What is the CAP and why was it created?**
> A: The Common Agricultural Policy is the EU's agricultural support system, launched in 1962. Originally created to: (1) guarantee food security post-WWII, (2) ensure fair incomes for farmers, (3) stabilize agricultural markets, (4) guarantee food supply at reasonable prices. It represents ~31-33% of the EU budget today (historically up to 70%). Managed by DG AGRI.

**Q2: What are the 2 pillars of the CAP? What does each one fund?**
> A: Pillar 1: Direct payments to farmers (income support based on land area) and market management measures — 100% EU-funded. Pillar 2: Rural Development (EAFRD) — supports environmental protection, farm modernization, rural infrastructure, organic farming, young farmers — co-financed by EU and member states. Pillar 1 = income support; Pillar 2 = structural transformation.

**Q3: What is "decoupling" and why was it introduced in the 2003 CAP reform?**
> A: Decoupling means that CAP payments are no longer linked to what or how much a farmer produces. Before 2003, payments were tied to production → perverse incentive to overproduce (creating "butter mountains," "grain lakes"). After the 2003 Fischler reform, farmers receive income support based on land area regardless of production decisions → they can produce what the market demands, not what maximizes subsidies.

**Q4: What is the "greening" component of the CAP?**
> A: Since the 2013 reform, 30% of direct payments (Pillar 1) are conditional on environmental practices: (1) crop diversification (2+ crops on farms over 10 ha), (2) maintaining permanent grassland, (3) having ecological focus areas (hedges, fallow land, nitrogen-fixing crops). In the 2023 CAP, "eco-schemes" replaced greening — voluntary for farmers but must be offered by states, with at least 25% of direct payments as the minimum envelope.

**Q5: Why did European farmers protest in 2024?**
> A: Multiple grievances: (1) Green Deal requirements (Farm to Fork: -50% pesticides, -20% fertilizers by 2030) making farming less profitable. (2) Unfair competition from cheaper non-EU imports (Ukrainian grain after war, South American beef) that don't meet EU standards. (3) Administrative burden of CAP rules. (4) Low farm incomes despite high food prices (supermarkets and processors capture most margin). The Commission partially responded by delaying/softening some F2F targets.

**Q6: What is the Farm to Fork Strategy and how does it relate to the Green Deal?**
> A: Farm to Fork (F2F) is a key component of the European Green Deal applied to the food system. Targets: reduce pesticide use by 50% by 2030, reduce fertilizer use by 20%, convert 25% of farmland to organic farming, reduce antibiotic use in livestock by 50%. Goal: more sustainable, healthy, and equitable food system. Critics argue targets are set without adequate impact assessments and ignore food security implications.

**Q7: How has the CAP evolved since its creation?**
> A: 1962: Price supports → overproduction. 1992 MacSharry: shift to direct aid. 1999 Agenda 2000: rural development as 2nd pillar. 2003 Fischler: decoupling of payments from production. 2013: greening (30% conditional on environmental practices), convergence of payment rates. 2023: New delivery model with national Strategic Plans, eco-schemes, stronger conditionality. Each reform reduced market distortions and added environmental requirements.

**Q8: What is the New Delivery Model of the CAP 2023-2027?**
> A: Member states now design their own CAP Strategic Plans (approved by the Commission) detailing how they will achieve the CAP's objectives. More flexibility to adapt to national conditions. But all plans must demonstrate contribution to 9 specific EU-level objectives (farm income, climate, biodiversity, young farmers, rural employment). Replaces the previous one-size-fits-all approach with a results-based framework.

**Q9: What is the WTO "green box" and why is it crucial for the CAP?**
> A: WTO rules categorize agricultural subsidies by their trade-distorting effect. "Amber box" subsidies (production-linked) are capped under WTO agreements. "Green box" subsidies (minimally trade-distorting) are unlimited. EU direct payments are classified as green box because they are decoupled from production — paying per hectare rather than per unit produced. This is why decoupling was essential: it made the CAP WTO-compatible.

**Q10: What are eco-schemes and how do they differ from the old "greening" payment?**
> A: Eco-schemes are voluntary payments to farmers who voluntarily go beyond the mandatory environmental baseline. Member states must offer them (mandatory for states), farmers choose whether to participate. They reward practices like precision farming, agroforestry, reduced tillage, wetland restoration — going further than greening's basic requirements. At least 25% of the Pillar 1 envelope must be devoted to eco-schemes.

**Q11: Why do many consider the CAP distribution unfair?**
> A: 80% of CAP subsidies go to 20% of farms (the largest). Large industrial farms in France, Germany, and Spain receive the most payments simply because they have the most land. Small family farms in Romania, Poland, and Mediterranean countries receive proportionally less. This incentivizes farm consolidation and rural depopulation. Reforms (capping, redistribution, degressivity) have addressed this partially but not eliminated the imbalance.

**Q12: How did the Ukraine war affect European agriculture and the CAP?**
> A: After Russia's 2022 invasion, the EU suspended import tariffs on Ukrainian agricultural goods to support Ukraine. This flooded markets in neighboring EU states (Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovakia) with cheap Ukrainian grain, sunflower, and poultry — collapsing local prices. EU farmers in those countries blocked borders. The Commission had to reimpose temporary safeguards and compensate affected farmers from the Agricultural Reserve.

**Q13: What is the EU-Mercosur trade deal and why do farmers oppose it?**
> A: The EU-Mercosur deal (with Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay) would eliminate tariffs on South American agricultural products (beef, sugar, chicken, soya). EU farmers oppose it because South American production doesn't meet EU standards (pesticide use, land clearing, animal welfare) — they see it as unfair competition that circumvents EU environmental rules. The deal has been negotiated since 1999 and still faces strong political resistance from France and others.

**Q14: What role does the CAP play in biodiversity protection?**
> A: The CAP contributes to biodiversity through: Pillar 1 — eco-schemes rewarding biodiversity-friendly practices (hedges, flower strips, reduced pesticides). Pillar 2 — agri-environment-climate measures (AECMs) paying farmers for managing land in ecologically sensitive ways. Integration with Natura 2000: farms within protected areas must be compatible with conservation objectives. The EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030 targets 30% of EU territory protected and restoration of degraded ecosystems.

**Q15: What challenges does the CAP face looking toward 2028?**
> A: Three major tensions: (1) Food security vs sustainability — Green Deal requirements reduce yields in the short term while global food demand grows. (2) Farm income vs budget constraints — political pressure to increase support while EU budget is squeezed. (3) Competitiveness vs standards — EU farmers face higher regulatory costs than global competitors. The next CAP reform must balance a green transition, fair farmer incomes, and food security — all while respecting WTO rules and EU budget limits.
