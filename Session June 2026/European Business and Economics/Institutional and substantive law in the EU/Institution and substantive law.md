# Institutional and Substantive Law in the EU
> How EU law is structured, created, and applied — and what it means for businesses

---

## PART 1 — COMPLETE SYNTHESIS

## KEY CONCEPT 1 — The EU Legal Order: Primary vs Secondary Law

EU law has a strict hierarchy:

```
Primary Law (founding treaties) — supreme source
        ↓
Secondary Law (regulations, directives, decisions...)
        ↓
National Law (must conform to EU law)
```

**Primary Law** = the EU's "constitution":
- **TEU** (Treaty on European Union): objectives, values, institutional structure
- **TFEU** (Treaty on the Functioning of the EU): detailed competences, policies, procedures
- **Charter of Fundamental Rights**: legally binding since Lisbon Treaty (2007)

**Key principle — Primacy of EU Law:** When there is a conflict between EU law and national law, EU law prevails. Established in *Costa v ENEL* (CJEU, 1964).

---

## KEY CONCEPT 2 — The 5 Types of Secondary Law

| Act | Binding? | Directly applicable? | Target |
|-----|----------|---------------------|--------|
| **Regulation** | Yes | Yes — applies automatically in all states | Everyone |
| **Directive** | Yes | No — states must transpose into national law | Member states (choose how) |
| **Decision** | Yes | Yes — but only for addressees | Specific states/companies/individuals |
| **Recommendation** | No | No | General guidance only |
| **Opinion** | No | No | Expression of a position |

**Classic exam trap:** Regulations do NOT need transposition (they are directly applicable). Directives DO need transposition (member states have a deadline, usually 2 years).

**Examples:**
- **Regulation**: GDPR (applies identically everywhere in the EU)
- **Directive**: USB-C charging standard (each state passes its own law implementing it)
- **Decision**: Commission fine on Google (targeted at Google specifically)

---

## KEY CONCEPT 3 — Direct Effect and Direct Applicability

Two distinct concepts often confused:

**Direct applicability** = the norm automatically becomes part of national law without any national act (only Regulations).

**Direct effect** = an individual can invoke EU law directly before a national court, even if the state hasn't implemented it.

**Conditions for direct effect** (established in *Van Gend en Loos*, 1963):
1. The provision must be clear and unconditional
2. The obligation must be complete and legally perfect
3. No further national implementing measure needed

**Horizontal vs vertical direct effect:**
- **Vertical**: citizen can invoke EU law against the state
- **Horizontal**: citizen can invoke EU law against another private party (more limited)

---

## KEY CONCEPT 4 — The 3 Principles of EU Competence

The EU can only act within the powers granted by the treaties:

| Principle | Definition | Consequence |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Conferral** | EU only has powers explicitly given by treaties | Cannot act outside its attribution |
| **Subsidiarity** | EU acts only if states cannot act effectively alone | Preference for action at the lowest effective level |
| **Proportionality** | EU measures must not go beyond what's needed | No regulatory overkill |

**Subsidiarity Protocol:** National parliaments can issue "yellow cards" if they believe the Commission is violating subsidiarity (Treaty of Lisbon innovation). If 1/3 of parliaments object → Commission must review the proposal.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 5 — How EU Law Is Enforced

**The CJEU (Court of Justice of the EU)** in Luxembourg has three functions:
1. **Preliminary rulings** (Article 267 TFEU): national courts can (sometimes must) ask the CJEU to interpret EU law
2. **Infringement proceedings** (Article 258 TFEU): Commission sues a member state for violating EU law
3. **Annulment actions** (Article 263 TFEU): challenging the legality of EU acts

**State liability** (*Francovich* case, 1991): If a state fails to implement a directive and a citizen suffers harm, the state must pay damages — even if the directive wasn't implemented.

**Key rulings to know:**
| Case | Year | What it established |
|------|------|---------------------|
| Van Gend en Loos | 1963 | Direct effect of EU law |
| Costa v ENEL | 1964 | Primacy of EU law over national law |
| Cassis de Dijon | 1979 | Mutual recognition principle |
| Francovich | 1991 | State liability for failing to implement directives |

---

## KEY CONCEPT 6 — The Single Market Legal Framework

The Single Market is built on 4 fundamental freedoms enshrined in the TFEU:

| Freedom | TFEU Articles | Core prohibition |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Free movement of goods** | Art. 34-36 | No quantitative restrictions or equivalent measures |
| **Free movement of services** | Art. 56-62 | No restrictions on providing services across borders |
| **Free movement of capital** | Art. 63-66 | No restrictions on capital movements and payments |
| **Free movement of persons** | Art. 45-55 | Free movement of workers + citizens (Directive 2004/38) |

**Cassis de Dijon principle (1979):** If a product is lawfully produced and sold in one EU state, it must generally be allowed in all others (mutual recognition). This avoids requiring harmonization for every product.

**Exceptions allowed** (Art. 36 TFEU — exhaustive list): public policy, public security, public health, protection of national treasures, industrial property.

## KEY CONCEPT 7 — Competition Law (Substantive Law)

Two key prohibitions in the TFEU that directly affect businesses:

**Article 101 TFEU — Anti-competitive agreements:**
- Prohibits agreements between companies that restrict competition
- Covers: price-fixing, market sharing, production limits, bid rigging
- Applies to: cartels, association decisions, concerted practices
- **Exemption** (Art. 101(3)): if agreement improves production/distribution AND benefits consumers AND competition isn't eliminated

**Article 102 TFEU — Abuse of dominant position:**
- Doesn't prohibit dominance itself — prohibits **abuse** of that dominant position
- Abuse examples: predatory pricing, exclusivity clauses, tying/bundling, margin squeeze
- "Dominant" = roughly 40%+ market share (case-by-case assessment)

**State Aid (Art. 107 TFEU):**
- Government subsidies that distort competition are generally prohibited
- Exceptions: regional development, R&D, services of general economic interest
- Must be notified to and approved by the Commission before implementation

## KEY CONCEPT 8 — EU Regulation of Business: Key Legislation

Modern EU law directly impacts how companies operate:

| Regulation/Directive | What it does | Business impact |
|---------------------|-------------|----------------|
| **GDPR** (2018) | Data protection rights | Fines up to 4% of global turnover |
| **CSRD** (2024) | Mandatory ESG reporting | Large listed companies must disclose sustainability data |
| **Digital Markets Act** (2022) | Regulates "gatekeepers" (Big Tech) | Google, Apple, Meta must open platforms |
| **AI Act** (2024) | Risk-based AI regulation | High-risk AI systems require conformity assessment |
| **USB-C Directive** | Universal charger standard | All devices must use USB-C from 2024 |

---

## KEY CONCEPT 9 — Fundamental Rights in the EU

**Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU** (legally binding since 2009, Lisbon Treaty):
- Dignity (Art. 1-5)
- Freedoms (Art. 6-19): expression, religion, assembly, property
- Equality (Art. 20-26): gender equality, disability, age
- Solidarity (Art. 27-38): workers' rights, social security, healthcare
- Citizens' rights (Art. 39-46): vote in EU elections, petition Parliament
- Justice (Art. 47-50): fair trial, right to defense, ne bis in idem

**ECHR vs EU Charter:** European Convention on Human Rights (Council of Europe, 46 states) ≠ EU Charter (EU law, 27 states). EU was considering acceding to the ECHR (Article 6 TEU), but CJEU Opinion 2/13 blocked this.

---

## KEY CONCEPT 10 — International Agreements and EU External Law

The EU concludes international agreements that become part of EU law:

- **Mixed agreements**: when subject matter falls partly within exclusive EU competence and partly within member state competence (e.g., CETA)
- **Exclusive agreements**: fully within EU competence (e.g., trade agreements under Art. 207 TFEU)

**Hierarchy when ratified:** International treaties ratified by the EU are above secondary law but below primary law (founding treaties).

**WTO law:** EU is a founding member; WTO agreements bind the EU and are applied through EU regulations.

## PART 2 — Q&A EXAM FORMAT

**Q1: What is the difference between Primary Law and Secondary Law in the EU?**
> A: Primary Law is the supreme source — the founding treaties (TEU and TFEU) plus the Charter of Fundamental Rights. Secondary Law is made under the authority of those treaties: regulations, directives, decisions, recommendations and opinions. In case of conflict, primary law always prevails. National law must conform to both.

**Q2: Explain the 5 types of secondary EU law and their binding force.**
> A: Regulation: binding, directly applicable in all states (no national law needed) — e.g., GDPR. Directive: binding on states as to the result, but states choose how to implement it (transposition required) — e.g., USB-C directive. Decision: binding on specific addressees only — e.g., Commission fine on a company. Recommendation: not binding, provides guidance. Opinion: not binding, expresses a position.

**Q3: What is the difference between direct applicability and direct effect?**
> A: Direct applicability means a legal act automatically becomes part of national law without any national implementing measure (only Regulations have this). Direct effect means an individual can invoke an EU legal provision directly before a national court. Established in Van Gend en Loos (1963): the provision must be clear, unconditional, and require no further implementation.

**Q4: What is the principle of primacy of EU law? Which case established it?**
> A: EU law takes precedence over all national law, including national constitutions, when they conflict. Established by the CJEU in Costa v ENEL (1964). If a national law contradicts EU law, national courts must set aside the national provision and apply EU law directly.

**Q5: What are the 3 principles governing EU competences? Explain each.**
> A: Conferral: the EU has only the powers explicitly attributed by the treaties — it cannot act beyond them. Subsidiarity: the EU acts only when member states cannot achieve objectives effectively alone; preference is given to action at the lowest effective level. Proportionality: EU measures must not exceed what is necessary to achieve the treaty objective.

**Q6: What is a preliminary ruling (Article 267 TFEU)?**
> A: A mechanism allowing (and sometimes requiring) national courts to ask the CJEU to interpret EU law before deciding a case. National courts of last instance MUST refer questions; lower courts may. The CJEU gives the ruling, then sends it back to the national court to apply. This ensures EU law is interpreted uniformly across all 27 states.

**Q7: What is the Cassis de Dijon principle and why is it important?**
> A: Established in 1979, this CJEU ruling introduced the principle of mutual recognition: if a product is lawfully manufactured and sold in one EU member state, it can generally be sold in all others without meeting additional national requirements. This dramatically reduced the need for full harmonization and accelerated Single Market integration.

**Q8: What does Article 101 TFEU prohibit?**
> A: Article 101 prohibits agreements between companies that restrict or distort competition within the EU — cartels, price-fixing, market sharing, production limits, bid rigging. These agreements are void. Exception (Art. 101(3)): agreements that improve production or distribution and pass benefits to consumers may be exempted if competition is not eliminated.

**Q9: What does Article 102 TFEU prohibit?**
> A: Article 102 prohibits the abuse of a dominant position within the EU. Dominance itself is not illegal — only its abuse. Abusive practices include: predatory pricing (below cost to drive out competitors), exclusivity arrangements, tying products together, or imposing unfair trading conditions. Example: Google fined €4.3B for forcing Android makers to pre-install its apps.

**Q10: What is State Aid and how does the EU control it?**
> A: State Aid is government financial support (subsidies, tax breaks, loans) granted to specific companies. Article 107 TFEU prohibits aid that distorts competition in the Single Market. States must notify the Commission before granting aid; the Commission decides if it's compatible. Exceptions exist for regional development, R&D, environmental protection.

**Q11: What are the 4 fundamental freedoms of the Single Market? Give the TFEU articles.**
> A: Free movement of goods (Art. 34-36). Free movement of services (Art. 56-62). Free movement of capital (Art. 63-66). Free movement of persons (Art. 45-55). Together these form the legal backbone of the internal market, making the EU function like one domestic market for trade and investment.

**Q12: What is the Francovich principle?**
> A: Established by the CJEU in 1991: if a member state fails to implement a directive within the deadline and a citizen suffers damage as a result, the state must pay compensation. Three conditions: the directive confers rights on individuals; those rights are clear from the directive's content; there is a causal link between the state's failure and the damage suffered.

**Q13: What is the Charter of Fundamental Rights and when did it become binding?**
> A: The Charter codifies fundamental rights applicable to EU institutions and member states when implementing EU law. It covers dignity, freedoms, equality, solidarity, citizens' rights, and justice. It became legally binding with the Treaty of Lisbon (2009). It applies to EU institutions always, and to member states only when they are acting within the scope of EU law.

**Q14: What is the GDPR and what are its key obligations for businesses?**
> A: The General Data Protection Regulation (2018) is an EU Regulation (directly applicable) that governs the processing of personal data. Key obligations: legal basis for processing, data minimization, right to access/erase/portability, data breach notification (72 hours), and appointment of a DPO for high-risk processing. Fines up to €20M or 4% of global annual turnover.

**Q15: How does the Digital Markets Act change the rules for large tech platforms?**
> A: The DMA (2022) designates large tech platforms as "gatekeepers" (Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft) based on size and dependency. Gatekeepers must: allow third-party app stores, not favor their own services in rankings, share data with business users, ensure interoperability. Fines up to 10% of global turnover (20% for repeat violations). Goal: contestable and fair digital markets.
